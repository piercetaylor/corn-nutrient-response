# Figure plan

Twelve figures. Each is listed with what it shows, what it is doing in the
argument that would be lost without it, and how to read it. A figure that only
restated a number in the text was not drawn; the treatment means, for instance,
appear as a table because nine numbers in a table are easier to compare than
nine positions on an axis, while their distributions are worth a figure because
spread and skew are not readable from a table.

All figures are generated by `analysis/run_all.R` at 150 dots per inch on a
white ground, through a single wrapper in `R/99_utils.R` so that they share
dimensions and typography.

---

**fig01_yield_by_treatment** — Boxplots of plot grain yield for each of the nine
standard treatments, with treatment means marked.

*Why it earns its place.* It is the first look at the result the whole
repository is about. The nitrogen-omission treatment sits visibly lower than the
other eight, and the other eight sit on top of each other. A reader who takes
nothing else from the analysis should take that contrast.

*How to read it.* Treatments are ordered by nitrogen rate, then by the
phosphorus and potassium variants. Boxes give the interquartile range across all
34 site-years, so their height is dominated by between-field variation, not by
treatment effect. Compare the positions of the boxes, not their overlap: the
paired contrasts in fig10 are the correct test.

---

**fig02_yield_distribution** — Histogram of plot grain yield with the mean
marked.

*Why it earns its place.* It answers a question the boxplots do not: whether the
marginal distribution of yield is well behaved. The left tail visible here is
the nitrogen-omission treatment, and it is the reason the residuals of the
plot-level analysis of variance fail a normality test. The figure makes that
diagnosis legible instead of leaving it as a p-value.

*How to read it.* The bulk of the distribution sits between twelve and sixteen
megagrams per hectare. The thin extension below ten is not measurement error.

---

**fig03_plant_correlations** — Correlation matrix of the fifteen plant
measurements, hierarchically ordered, with coefficients printed.

*Why it earns its place.* It establishes the redundancy that motivates both the
principal-components section and the exclusion of harvest measurements from the
yield models. Total dry matter, nitrogen uptake, potassium uptake and sulfur
uptake form a visible block, because each is a concentration multiplied by a
mass that includes grain.

*How to read it.* Blue is positive, red negative. Read the blocks rather than
individual cells; the hierarchical ordering puts variables that move together
next to each other.

---

**fig04_yield_vs_n_uptake_by_texture** — Grain yield against nitrogen uptake,
one panel per soil texture class, each with a fitted line.

*Why it earns its place.* This is the Simpson's paradox check drawn rather than
tabulated. The pooled coefficient could in principle be an artefact of pooling
fields with different soils; the panels show that it is not, because the slope
is positive and of similar magnitude in every class.

*How to read it.* Compare slopes across panels, not intercepts. Panels differ
greatly in sample size: the silt loam class carries 17 of the 34 site-years and
the sandy loam class carries five, so the fitted lines are not equally well
determined.

---

**fig05_site_years** — The 34 site-years on latitude and longitude, sized by
irrigation applied and coloured by mean yield.

*Why it earns its place.* It gives the reader the physical layout of the
experiment in one view and shows the west-to-east gradient in applied
irrigation, which is the single strongest site-level pattern in the data and the
reason longitude behaves as a proxy for water in any model that admits it.

*How to read it.* Larger points received more irrigation. Longitude runs from
about minus 104 in the west to minus 96 in the east; irrigation falls as
growing-season rainfall rises eastward.

---

**fig06_nitrogen_response** — Grain yield against applied nitrogen, with grey
points for site-year means within each treatment and a red line joining the
treatment means.

*Why it earns its place.* The nitrogen response is the reference against which
the phosphorus and potassium effects are judged, and its shape matters: it is
steep to the mid rate and nearly flat beyond. Reporting a single nitrogen
coefficient would hide the curvature.

*How to read it.* Each grey point is one site-year under one treatment, so the
vertical scatter at a given rate is between-field variation. The red line is the
average response, not a fitted model.

---

**fig07_pca_scree** — Eigenvalues of the fifteen principal components, with the
retained components shaded and a line at an eigenvalue of one.

*Why it earns its place.* It shows the retention decision being made by the
stated criterion rather than by inspection of a bend in a curve. Four components
sit above the line and eleven below it, and there is no ambiguity to resolve by
eye.

*How to read it.* The dashed line is the average eigenvalue for standardised
variables. Bars above it are retained under Kaiser's criterion.

---

**fig08_pca_scores** — Plots in the plane of the first two components, coloured
by treatment.

*Why it earns its place.* It shows what the components do not do. If the
dominant axes of variation among plant measurements corresponded to the
treatments, the colours would separate. They do not, except for the
nitrogen-omission treatment. The components describe crop size and partitioning,
which vary far more between fields than between the eight fertilised treatments.

*How to read it.* Look for colour separation and note its absence. The one
colour that does separate is the treatment that received no nitrogen.

---

**fig09_clusters** — Cluster membership against field position, jittered, with
the measured associations in the subtitle.

*Why it earns its place.* Clustering is often reported as though the clusters
were discovered groups. Here the figure and its subtitle together say what the
clusters are: they track which field a plot was in far more closely than what
was applied to it, and the Cramer's V values quantify by how much.

*How to read it.* Points are jittered because plots within a site-year share one
coordinate pair. Read the spatial coherence of colour, not individual points.

---

**fig10_treatment_contrasts** — The five pre-specified contrasts as estimates
with 95 percent confidence intervals, on a common axis in megagrams per hectare.

*Why it earns its place.* This is the result. It puts the nitrogen response and
the phosphorus and potassium effects on one scale, so that the reader sees not
only that the latter are not significant but that they are small: the intervals
exclude anything approaching agronomic importance, which a table of p-values
would not have shown.

*How to read it.* The vertical line is zero. An interval crossing it is
consistent with no effect; how far the interval extends from zero is the more
useful reading, because it bounds the effect that could have been missed.

---

**fig11_observed_vs_predicted** — Predicted against observed grain yield for the
held-out site-years, on equal axes, with the line of equality.

*Why it earns its place.* It shows the character of the prediction error, not
just its size. Points compress toward the training mean, which is what a model
does when the predictors carry little information about the response, and the
compression is visible in a way that a single summary statistic is not.

*How to read it.* The line is equality, not a fit. Axes are equal and share
limits, so vertical distance from the line is the error in megagrams per
hectare.

---

**fig12_variable_importance** — Random forest permutation importance for the
twelve most important predictors.

*Why it earns its place.* It identifies what little signal the ex-ante
predictors carry, and it agrees with the experimental result: applied nitrogen
dominates, and the phosphorus and potassium rates rank below every soil-test
variable. Two independent methods reaching the same ordering is worth showing.

*How to read it.* The statistic is the increase in out-of-bag mean squared error
when the variable is permuted. It ranks predictors within this model; it is not
an effect size and it does not carry a sign.
